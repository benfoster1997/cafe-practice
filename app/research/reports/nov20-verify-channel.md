# Fact-check of the channel and law report (20 November 2026)

File checked: `app/research/reports/nov20-channel-law.md`. Its verdict was **LAWFUL_WITH_CHANGES**.

This is a check of one report. It is not a search for new ideas. It is research, not legal advice. I am not a lawyer.

Source labels: **primary** (the law, the ICO, official statistics, a firm's own price list), **independent** (press, polls, first-hand posts), **vendor_or_seo** (sales-software firms: direction only), **inference** (my own reasoning).

## Bottom line

1. **The law in the report is right.** I opened the ICO's own pages and the regulations today and compared the wording. I found no legal statement that is wrong.
2. **The verdict stands, with corrections.** The corrections are: one gap in the email rule, one untested point about the screening seller, and one claim about results that is firmer than the evidence.
3. **The weak part is the forecast, not the law.** Nobody has measured how UK tradespeople respond to a cold call. The report's "5 to 25 conversations" is a guess built on sales-software figures. By the report's own lowest inputs the answer is 4.5, which is under the gate of 5.
4. **One person is not too small to be fined.** The ICO fined a sole trader £50,000 in May 2025. That was for 194,110 calls, about a thousand times the size of this test. I found no action over a few hundred calls.

## How this was checked

- 10 web searches (the full budget), using different queries from the report.
- Pages I opened and read today: six ICO pages, PECR regulations 21 and 24, the fee regulations Schedule, the 2026 business population release, both Belkins studies, the screening seller's price page, a YouGov poll, and one ICO fine notice.
- Seen as search extracts only: the commencement order for the 2025 Act, the ICO's statement on it, the Instantly and Smartlead email figures, two further ICO fines.
- One search (official TPS licence price) returned only unrelated American results. That figure is still **not found**.

### Claim 3: the gap in the email rule

The report says a firm found on Companies House can be emailed. That is not quite enough. What matters is who owns the email address, not only what the firm is.

The ICO says the position "would be different" where addresses belong to individual subscribers, "because they belong to sole traders or because the delegate chose to use their personal email address instead of their work one" (ICO business-to-business page, primary).

Many small trade companies use a personal Gmail or Hotmail style address. The ICO page says nothing about those addresses directly. Its general advice is that if you are unsure, "you should treat the details as belonging to an individual subscriber". So the safe reading (inference) is: only email a limited company at an address on its own business domain. If the only address looks personal, phone instead.

A second point the report did not spell out. The ICO lists "contacting people to ask them for consent to direct marketing" as direct marketing in itself (ICO, Identify direct marketing, primary). So he cannot email or text a sole trader to ask for permission. He can only ask on a lawful phone call.

### Claim 6: the phone forecast

What holds up:

- Belkins: 9.9% of dials answered, 24.5% of people reached after about three tries, 58% of answered calls get past the greeting, UK 14.7% against US 9.0%. All quoted correctly. Source: [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-calling-benchmarks) (vendor_or_seo).

What weakens it:

- Belkins itself says the UK figure comes from a smaller sample and is "directional". The UK sample size is not given.
- The callers were professional sales staff using a dialling tool. The people called were managers and directors in offices. None were tradespeople.
- The only independent UK figure I found: 69% of British adults say they would not typically answer a call from an unknown number. 28% say they would. Source: [YouGov, 6,315 adults, 14 May 2025](https://yougov.com/en-gb/daily-results/20250514-e27de-3) (independent). This is the general public, and it is what people say, not what was measured. It fits a pick-up rate somewhere between 10% and 28%.
- The step that decides the result is unmeasured: how many people who pick up will give a stranger two minutes. The report assumes a quarter to a half. No source supports that.
- The report's own lowest inputs give 200 x 9.0% x 25% = 4.5 conversations. That is under the gate. The report's range starts at 5.
- Screening will remove some numbers. How many: **not found**. One vendor says over 18.7 million numbers are registered ([Data8](https://www.data-8.co.uk/?p=20611), vendor_or_seo, extract). The seller in the report says about 23 million. The two do not agree.
- The Fix Radio survey of tradespeople could not be traced to an original. I found it only through the same vendor blog. **Unverifiable.**

Honest restatement (inference): one dial each to 200 firms gives roughly 3 to 15 conversations. With a second try, roughly 5 to 25. The result could land either side of 5. The verdict's phrase "should give enough conversations to pass the gate" is too firm. "May" is the honest word.

### Claim 7: the email forecast

- Belkins: 0.45% reply per email sent, from 7,530,489 emails in 2025. UK 0.48%. Firms of 0 to 10 staff 0.72%. Construction sits in a 0.56% to 0.60% band. All quoted correctly. Source: [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates) (vendor_or_seo).
- Other vendors count per campaign and get higher numbers. Instantly: 3.43% average, 5.5% for the top quarter of senders, 10.7% for the top tenth. Smartlead: 0.74%, top quarter 1.43%. Sources: [Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026), [Smartlead](https://www.smartlead.ai/benchmarks) (both vendor_or_seo, extracts).
- On 200 firms that is 1 to 7 replies for a typical sender and about 20 for a top-tenth sender. If half of replies are real answers, that is 0 to 4 conversations typically and about 10 at the very top (inference).
- So "likely under 5" is fair. "Under 5 whatever the effort" is not supported. The report's own wording ("can score under 5 with perfect effort") is the accurate version.

## Smaller points checked

- **ICO fee.** "Most companies will only need to pay £52 or £78 a year", £5 off by Direct Debit. Confirmed. Source: [ICO fee page](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/getting-started-with-gdpr/data-protection-fee-what-you-need-to-do/) (primary). The exemption for "advertising, marketing and public relations in respect of the data controller's business" is in the law. Confirmed. Source: [fee regulations Schedule](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/480/schedule) (primary). Whether the owner is exempt is the report's inference. Unverifiable. Its advice to run the fee checker is sound.
- **Screening price.** £5.00 plus VAT for up to 250 numbers, £7.00 plus VAT for 500. Confirmed on the seller's page. But the page does not say the seller holds a TPS licence, and does not say one check covers both lists. It says the service is "best endeavours" and "as is". Source: [TPS Services](https://www.tpsservices.co.uk/tps-services-for-businesses/tps-services-pricing.aspx) (primary for its own prices). **Weakened.** He must get both points confirmed in writing before he pays, because the screening receipt is his main proof.
- **Consent given on the phone.** Valid, if recorded. ICO: "If consent was given orally, your records should include a copy of the script used at that time" and "a note of the time and date which was made at the time of the conversation." Consent "should be specific and granular". Source: [ICO consent guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/how-should-we-obtain-record-and-manage-consent/) (primary). The report's closing line asks about "ring or email" in one breath. Ask the two separately.
- **Fines on small operators.** The report listed company fines of £100,000 to £240,000. It missed that sole traders are fined too: £50,000 for 194,110 calls ([ICO, 15 May 2025](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/05/sole-trader-fined-50-000-after-making-over-194-000-unlawful-marketing-calls/), primary) and £200,000 for nearly one million texts ([ICO, October 2025](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/10/200-000-fine-for-sole-trader-who-sent-nearly-one-million-spam-texts), primary, extract). The ICO said it will act "from sole trader to larger companies". The smallest case I found was 95,000 calls (£90,000, April 2025, extract). Any action over a few hundred calls: **not found**.

## Is the reasoning sound?

- **Vendor sources treated as fact:** no. The report labels them. But its verdict sentence leans on them harder than its own caveats allow.
- **US data applied to the UK:** yes, for both phone and email, and it says so. No UK trades measurement exists that I could find.
- **Old data:** the 2023 forum thread, the 2023 fine, the 2024 Everreach survey and a three-year-old US post. All are marked and none carries the verdict.
- **Numbers with no source:** "20 dials an hour" comes from the earlier P3 report, which took it from vendors. The "quarter to a half" talk rate is the report's own assumption and it says so.
- **Too rosy or too gloomy:** the law is stated fairly. The phone forecast is slightly rosy at the bottom end. The fine ceiling of £17.5 million is correct but could frighten more than it should: real fines found were £50,000 to £240,000 for operations hundreds of times larger.
- **A correction to the older P3 report, which this report got right:** a paid VoIP line is not a legal requirement. An ordinary mobile with the number showing meets regulation 21.

## Corrected verdict

**LAWFUL_WITH_CHANGES** (unchanged).

Corrected reason: phoning numbers screened against TPS and CTPS is lawful for one person, for sole traders and companies alike. This is confirmed against the ICO's own words. Whether it produces 5 conversations is not known. The script line "I'm not selling anything" is sugging as the plan stands. Email is closed to sole traders, and also to any company that only has a personal-looking address.

## The exact rules for the 14-day test

1. **Screen first.** Before any dial, check every number against both the TPS and the CTPS. Get written confirmation that the checker is licensed and covers both lists. Keep the result. Make every call within 28 days of the check. Never call a listed number or anyone who has asked not to be called.
2. **Be identifiable.** Do not withhold your number. Say your full name at the start of every call. Give a postal address or a free-to-call number if asked.
3. **Tell the truth about why you are calling.** Say you are setting up a paid quote-writing service and want to ask one question. Do not say "I'm not selling anything". Do not call it research.
4. **Email only real company addresses.** Only firms confirmed on Companies House as a limited company or LLP, and only at a business-domain address, not a personal Gmail or Hotmail style one. Every email carries your name, a contact address and an easy way to say stop.
5. **No cold email, text, WhatsApp or social media message to a sole trader or partnership.** Not even to ask permission. Ask on the call instead, once for calls and once for email. Write down who, when and the words you used. Keep a do-not-contact list and obey it at once.
6. **Before the first call:** run the ICO fee checker and pay if unsure (£52, or £47 by Direct Debit). Write one page on why you are contacting these firms and why it is fair to them. Be ready to say where you got each number.

## The strongest argument against the report

The report says the email-led gate is unfair because a man could do everything right and still score under 5. The same is true of the phone-led gate it puts in its place. Its own lowest inputs give 4.5. The number 5 was never measured against anything. So a result of 3 or 4 after 200 honest calls would not show that the owner cannot sell. It would only show that the guess was high. The test is better read by what he controls: did he make the 200 calls, and how did day 10 feel against day 1. The conversation count should be treated as information, not as a pass mark.
