# Fact-check of the two "no cold outreach" reports (20 November 2026)

Files checked:

- `app/research/reports/nov20-no-selling-products.md`. Its verdict was **NO_TYPICAL_PAYOFF**.
- `app/research/reports/nov20-inbound-work.md`. Its verdict was **INBOUND_NOT_BETTER**.

This is a check of two reports. It is not a search for new ideas and it proposes none. It is research, not legal advice.

Source labels: **primary** (a regulator, the law, official statistics, a company's own filing, terms or data), **independent** (press, academic work, first-hand posts), **vendor_or_seo** (marketing blogs and sites that sell something: direction only), **inference** (my own reasoning).

"Search extract only" means I could not open the page and am relying on the short extract the search returned.

## Bottom line

1. **Both verdicts stand, with corrections.** I tried to prove the main numbers wrong and could not. Most were confirmed at the original source.
2. **One claim is out of date and now wrong.** The products report says Etsy's buyers are shrinking. That was true a year ago. Etsy's latest results (July to September 2026) show buyers up 1.0%, sellers up 6.5% and sales up 8.8%. This does not rescue Etsy as an income route. Average sales per seller are still about £120 a month before fees.
3. **Each verdict line leans on one old number.** The Gumroad figure is from 2020. The "1 in 9 ever got work" figure is from a 2021 study of registered profiles, not of people who actually tried. Both numbers are quoted correctly. Neither is current.
4. **The part-time job comparison is not like for like.** The inbound report sets 18 hours a week of paid work against a plan that needs far fewer hours. On the same hours the job still comes out ahead in the first six months, but by about £100 to £350 a month, not by "a wide margin".
5. **No no-outreach route for Claude-assisted work beats the standing pick on the evidence.** The only thing that beats it is ordinary paid work, which is not a Claude income. Details and how sure we can be are in the last two sections.

## How this was checked

- 12 web searches (the full budget), using different queries from the two reports.
- Pages I opened and read today: RevenueCat's 2026 report page, the academic paper behind "1 in 9", Etsy's own letter to shareholders for the third quarter of 2026, Articore's results announcement, the Acas minimum wage page, section 13 of the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982, Mindrift's eligibility page, the Lancaster University remote-jobs release, a Brookings article, and press reports on Etsy, Fiverr, the Claude Marketplace, Mercor and AI-written code.
- Could not open: Articore's full annual report (the file was too large, and a direct download was refused in this session), Gumroad's own 2020 post (only the title loaded), and an OpenAI and Upwork study page (blocked).
- I did not look up exchange rates. I reuse the reports' rough rates: about 1.30 US dollars and about 2 Australian dollars to the pound (inference, same as the reports).

### Errors and overstatements

1. **The Etsy trend is a year out of date.** See row 7. The report had the latest figures available to it and used last year's. The correction cuts both ways:
   - Buyers are no longer falling.
   - Sellers are growing faster than buyers (6.5% against 1.0%).
   - Average sales per active seller are unchanged at about $1,900 a year, about £120 a month, before Etsy's fees and the seller's costs (inference: $126 per buyer x 87.5 million buyers / 5.8 million sellers).
   - Etsy also said its search had sometimes favoured cheap digital items and that it is now adjusting "the prominence of physical versus digital products". That is bad news for download sellers ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/etsy-q3-2026-earnings/), independent, opened).
2. **The verdict line calls a Redbubble average "the middle seller".** £22 a year is the average among artists who sold something. The middle artist earns less than the average. So the wording is loose, and the real picture is worse, not better (inference). "Net artist earnings" also appears to be after Redbubble's own account fees (search extract only).
3. **RevenueCat's sample has a floor the report did not mention.** The page says apps must "meet a minimum threshold of installs or revenue". Apps that never got going are left out. So the true middle app earns less than $72 a month (inference).
4. **Gumroad's $70 is 2020 data in a 2026 verdict.** It is quoted correctly. It should be labelled as old every time it is used. One blog lists 1,853 Gumroad sellers above $10,000 a year, which would be about 4 in 100 of the 45,917, but it does not say which year ([Blogging Guide](https://bloggingguide.com/?p=27284), vendor_or_seo, search extract only). Treat as unverified.
5. **"About the same number of buyers" in every family is too sweeping.** Proven for apps. For WordPress plugins and Shopify apps the report shows more sellers but gives no buyer figures. For Etsy in 2026 it is not true.

None of these changes the answer. Every confirmed figure is far below £500 a month for the typical seller, and every sample leaves out the people who earned nothing.

### Corrected verdict for report 1

**NO_TYPICAL_PAYOFF. Stands.** Corrected wording: "In every family with real data, the typical seller earns a small fraction of £500 a month. No source measures newcomers with no audience at all, so their exact odds are unknown, but every sample is tilted towards people doing better than a newcomer. The flood of new sellers is proven for phone apps. Etsy's buyers were falling in 2025 and are growing slightly again in 2026."

### Claim 9: the job comparison, redone on equal hours

"Expected value" means what a route pays on average once you allow for the chance that it pays nothing.

The report sets a job of 18 hours a week (about 78 hours a month) against the pick. But the pick needs fewer hours. `STATUS.md` says about 10 hours a week at the start. The model in `strategy-P3-service-first.md` section 3 shows 20 to 28 hours a month in months 1 to 3 and 30 to 38 hours a month in months 4 to 6. The two repo files do not agree with each other.

| Hours | A job at £12.71 an hour, before tax (my arithmetic) | The pick over the same months, on the report's own sum |
|---|---|---|
| 20 to 28 a month (months 1 to 3 in the model) | about £255 to £355 a month | about £0 to £140 a month (35% x £0 to £400) |
| 30 to 38 a month (months 4 to 6 in the model) | about £380 to £485 a month | about £280 to £420 a month (35% x £800 to £1,200) |
| 10 a week (the `STATUS.md` figure) | about £550 a month | as above |
| 18 a week (the report's headline) | about £990 a month | as above |

What this shows (inference):

- On equal hours the job is still ahead in the first six months. The gap is about £100 to £350 a month, not £600 or more.
- The job's real advantage is certainty. The wage is set by law. The pick's figures are guesses from the repo.
- The pick's side of the sum is probably too high. The 35% is the repo's guess that he keeps selling for 18 months. The "2 to 3 clients by months 4 to 6" is a base case that assumes it works. And `nov20-verify-pick.md`, written today, found that demand at £395 is unproven. So the true gap may be wider than my table shows. Nobody can measure it from a desk.
- After month six the model has the pick paying £1,200 to £2,000 for 31 to 43 hours a month, if it works. That would be about £28 to £65 an hour, well above the minimum wage. "If it works" is the unproven part.

So the fair sentence is: "A job wins on certainty, and on expected value for at least the first six months. Beyond that, the evidence does not say."

### Other errors and overstatements

1. **"For most new accounts the honest figure is never"** goes further than the evidence. It is fair for accounts in general, because most are never used. For a person who pitches every week, the odds are not known (see row 1).
2. **"Breaking Even" is labelled independent.** It is one site and newsletter that tracks gig platforms. Its counts of Reddit complaint threads cannot be checked. Treat it as direction only. The report's key point about AI bans no longer depends on it, because Mercor's own statement now confirms it.
3. **The verdict reads as "inbound is worse".** What the evidence supports is narrower: "there is no evidence that inbound is better". Both sides of the comparison are unmeasured for a newcomer in 2026.
4. **Exchange rates and the "40 to 50 pitches" figure have no solid source.** The report says so itself. Neither carries the verdict.
5. **Not re-checked today:** Bark's credit price, the pay rates quoted by DataAnnotation and Outlier, and the rule that a sole trader is personally liable for business debts (general knowledge; the official site is blocked here).

## Did either report err in the wrong direction?

**Too gloomy?** I looked for real figures that either report ignored.

- The one I found is Etsy's 2026 recovery (above). It does not lift the typical seller near the bar.
- The biggest paid minority in either report is real and is stated: about 1 subscription app in 6 reaches $1,000 a month within two years. I confirmed it. It comes from a sample with a size floor, it includes funded teams, and it fell from 19% to 17% in a year. It is the nearest thing to a counter-example and it is in the next-to-last section.
- I found no dataset in which a typical newcomer with no audience reaches £500 a month.

**Too rosy?** Neither report rests a hopeful claim on a seller-coach blog. Where marketing blogs carry weight, they carry gloomy or neutral figures: the Fiverr newcomer estimate, Upwork pitch counts, the 6% hire rate, Bark's cost per job won, and the Shopify model. All are labelled. The only one that feeds a conclusion is the 6% hire rate (row 10), and that conclusion is marked unverifiable here.

**US data applied to the UK.** Almost every figure is global or American. Both reports say so. These are worldwide platforms, so a UK seller meets the same crowd (inference). The UK-only facts are the minimum wage, the remote-jobs share and the 1982 Act. All three were confirmed.

**Old data.** Gumroad (2020), the "1 in 9" study (2021), the Upwork studies (2023 data), and the Chrome cross-check I used myself (2019).

## Does any no-outreach route beat or usefully complement the standing pick?

Plainly: **no route for Claude-assisted income does.** One thing outside that question does.

| Route | Beats the pick? | Useful alongside it? | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Products sold on platforms (apps, templates, books, print-on-demand, extensions) | No | No. They compete for the same evenings and the typical result is a few pounds a month. | Fairly sure. Several primary datasets agree, and all of them flatter a newcomer. |
| Freelance marketplaces (Upwork, Fiverr, Bark and similar) | Not shown | Not shown. A free Fiverr listing costs nothing but an evening. Expect £0. | Sure of the direction (cheap work is draining away). Not sure of the size. No newcomer has been measured. |
| AI-training gig work | Not a Claude income. Outside AI is forbidden. | Possibly, as a free application for a small side income done by hand. | Sure about the AI ban at Mercor and the project rules at Mindrift. Not sure at all about the odds of being accepted or the hours on offer. |
| Ordinary paid work (part-time job, or extra hours in his own field) | Yes, on certainty and on the first six months | It is the fallback if he will not contact strangers. `STATUS.md` already says so. | Sure about the hourly floor, which is set by law. His job, his free hours and whether he would be hired are unknown. |

One caution about the bar itself. Today's other check (`nov20-verify-pick.md`) found that demand for the pick at £395 is unproven. So "nothing beats the pick" is a low bar. It mostly means that nothing here is proven, and the only number in the whole comparison that is certain is the hourly wage.

## The strongest argument against this check's conclusion

The pick that everything is measured against is itself unproven as of today. So "not better than the pick" says little. An owner who will not keep contacting strangers (the repo's own guess is that about 65% of the time he will not) gets nothing from the pick. For him, a route that costs nothing and has any chance at all could be worth more per hour: a free marketplace listing, a free AI-training application, or a Claude-built subscription app, where a real dataset shows about 1 app in 6 reaching $1,000 a month within two years. Nobody has measured a newcomer's results on any of these in 2026, so the reports cannot rule this out.

Why it does not overturn the verdicts (inference):

- The app figure comes from a sample with a size floor that includes funded teams. The middle app in that same sample makes about £55 a month after a year. The share reaching $1,000 is falling.
- Eight software ideas have already failed in this repo for the same reason: nobody finds an unknown seller's product.
- A subscription app takes payments and holds user data, which is exactly the kind of work the inbound report says a non-coder cannot check.
- The argument shows that the pick is weak. It does not show that anything else is strong. The certain alternative is still a wage.
