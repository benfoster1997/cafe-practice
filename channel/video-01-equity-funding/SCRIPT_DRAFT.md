## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:45)

In March 1973, investigators walked into one of America's most admired
financial companies looking for its customers.

Not the money. The customers.

Because this company — a Wall Street darling worth hundreds of millions —
had roughly sixty-four thousand policyholders who did not exist. A computer
had invented them. Names. Ages. Policy numbers. Two billion dollars of life
insurance, written on human beings who had never been born.

And here's the part that still gets taught in fraud classes fifty years
later: when the company needed even more money… it started killing them.

Everyone in this story is real. Except sixty-four thousand of them. This is
the story of Equity Funding — the first great fraud of the computer age.

## ACT ONE — THE MACHINE (0:45–4:00)

Los Angeles, 1960. A company called Equity Funding Corporation of America
opens its doors with a genuinely clever idea — and a completely legal one.

It worked like this. You buy shares in a mutual fund. Then you borrow
against those shares to pay the premiums on a life insurance policy. If the
fund grows faster than the loan, the investment pays for your insurance —
and you pocket the difference. One product. Two sales. Wall Street loved
it.

Two men ran the show: Stanley Goldblum — a big, imposing former
scrap-metal man with a taste for order — and Michael Riordan, the
charismatic dealmaker investors adored.

Then, in January 1969, the story takes its first dark turn. A winter storm
hits Los Angeles. In Mandeville Canyon, a wall of mud crashes through
Michael Riordan's home and kills him where he lived. His brother would
later become mayor of the city. Goldblum now runs Equity Funding alone.

And Equity Funding has a problem. It is a growth stock. Growth stocks must
grow. Every quarter, Wall Street expects the line to go up — and the honest
numbers won't cooperate.

So somewhere inside the company, a decision is made. If real customers
won't appear fast enough… the computer will make some.

Fake policies begin flowing into the books, hidden in a block the staff
called Department 99. To the mainframe, a fake person looks exactly like a
real one — a name, a birth date, a premium. And in the early 1970s, an
insurance company's books *were* its computer. Auditors couldn't see
people. They could only see printouts.

Now, inventing customers creates paper profits. But Equity Funding found a
way to turn fake people into real cash. It's called reinsurance: selling
your policies on to bigger insurance companies for money up front. Equity
Funding sold its phantom policyholders by the thousand — and the cash was
completely real.

But this created a treadmill. A reinsured policy owes premiums next year.
Fake customers don't pay premiums. So every year, the computer had to
invent even more people to cover the ones it invented last year. The fraud
had a growth target of its own.

And then came the darkest efficiency of all. A dead policyholder doesn't
owe premiums. A dead policyholder pays out. So Equity Funding began
reporting deaths among its phantom customers — collecting death benefits
from reinsurers on people who had never lived. Case studies of the fraud
describe plans to retire fake policyholders on a schedule — an actuarial
table for a population that didn't exist.

When auditors asked to sample policy files, staff simply forged the files —
sometimes overnight, to order. By the end, this wasn't one rogue
accountant. Investigators concluded that dozens of employees — by some
accounts as many as a hundred — knew, helped, or looked away. The eventual
indictment would even include charges of electronic eavesdropping. This
was, quite literally, a criminal organisation with a ticker symbol.

Nine years. That's how long it ran. Through audits, through regulators,
through one of the most sophisticated financial markets on earth.

It wasn't brought down by any of them.

## ACT TWO — THE TWO WEEKS (4:00–7:30)

Early March 1973. A mid-level insurance officer named Ronald Secrist has
just been let go from one of Equity Funding's subsidiaries. He knows what's
in Department 99. And he decides to talk.

But here's the detail that makes this story extraordinary — and it comes
straight from the federal court record. Secrist did NOT go to the SEC,
America's stock-market regulator. Why? Because, as he later told the
courts, he'd heard that employee tips to the SEC had found their way
straight back to Equity Funding's president. He didn't trust the referee.

So on March the sixth, 1973, he phones a man named Ray Dirks instead — a
39-year-old insurance analyst in New York. The next day, for several hours,
Secrist lays it all out. The court record calls his allegations "detailed
but nearly incredible" — tens of thousands of fake policies, forged files,
and claims that executives had threatened employees who objected.

Dirks does what the regulators never did. He gets on a plane to Los Angeles
and starts asking questions. Management denies everything. But employees —
one after another — confirm it.

Dirks then hands the scoop of the decade to the Wall Street Journal's Los
Angeles bureau chief. And the Journal… declines. The court record is blunt
about why: the bureau chief simply did not believe a fraud that enormous
could have gone undetected — and feared printing it would be libel.

The truth was sitting on a newsroom desk, too big to be believed.

But Dirks's clients believed it. As he investigated, the big funds he
advised quietly dumped their shares. In two weeks, Equity Funding stock
fell from twenty-six dollars to under fifteen. The market was screaming
what the newspapers wouldn't print.

On March the twenty-seventh, the New York Stock Exchange halted the stock.
California insurance examiners impounded the company's records — and went
looking for Department 99's policyholders. They found printouts. They found
premiums. They did not find people.

On April the second — only now — the SEC filed its complaint, and the Wall
Street Journal finally ran the story, on its front page. Days later,
Equity Funding collapsed into bankruptcy. Fortune magazine reported
investor losses around three hundred million dollars — and called it, at
the time, one of the largest bankruptcies in American history.

The empire of invented people was gone in under a month.

## ACT THREE — THE RECKONING, AND THE TWIST (7:30–10:00)

That November, a federal grand jury indicted twenty-two people on a
hundred and five counts — securities fraud, mail fraud, bank fraud,
counterfeit securities, even the eavesdropping. The auditors were in the
dock too: partners from the very firm that had signed off the books.

Stanley Goldblum pleaded guilty. In March 1975 he was sentenced to eight
years in prison — and fined twenty thousand dollars. Twenty thousand.
Against a two-billion-dollar phantom empire. He served four years.

But here is the twist this story is really remembered for.

Having missed the fraud for nine years, America's regulator finally took
decisive action… against Ray Dirks. The analyst. The man who exposed it.
The SEC charged that by telling his clients what Secrist told him, Dirks
had passed on inside information. And in its own censure order, the SEC
admitted — in writing — that Dirks had, quote, "played an important role
in bringing the massive fraud to light."

They punished the fire alarm.

Dirks refused to accept it. He fought for ten years — all the way to the
United States Supreme Court. And on July the first, 1983, the Court ruled
in his favour, six votes to three. The whistleblowers, the Court said, had
sought no profit — their motive was to expose the fraud. The rule written
in that judgment still defines American insider-trading law today. It is
called, simply, Dirks versus SEC.

As for the others — the afterlives are their own kind of story. Goldblum,
from behind the walls of his conviction, sued a television network to stop
a film about the scandal — filed twenty-four hours before broadcast. He
lost. The film aired in prime time, with a young James Woods playing the
company's actuary. Decades later, Goldblum's name surfaced again — charged
in a medical billing fraud at another company. He died in Beverly Hills in
2011, aged eighty-four. Ray Dirks died in 2023, aged eighty-nine — his
obituary led with the case that vindicated him. And Ronald Secrist, the
man who actually blew the whistle? He gave his testimony… and vanished
from history. The man who started it all is the one nobody remembers.

## OUTRO (10:00–10:40)

In 1973, faking sixty-four thousand people took a room-sized mainframe,
forged files, and as many as a hundred accomplices — and it still collapsed
in two weeks, because one fired employee made one phone call to one person
who checked.

Today, a laptop can invent a face, a voice, a paper trail, in seconds.

The machines have become better liars. The question is whether the
fire alarms have gotten better too — and whether, next time, anyone
answers the phone.

being researched — a Victorian fraudster who built a ballroom at the bottom
