## 1. TIMELINE

- **1960** — Equity Funding Corporation of America (EFCA) founded in Los Angeles. Gordon C. McCormick devises the founding concept; Stanley Goldblum and Michael Riordan become the two top officials and shareholders. [MoneyWeek][Wiki-EF]
- **The legitimate product** — the "funding program": a customer buys mutual-fund shares, then borrows against them to pay life-insurance premiums; if the fund outgrows the loan interest, the customer gets insurance cover "free" plus money left over. [MoneyWeek]
- **1964** — EFCA's first public offering. The bankruptcy Trustee later found the fraud "began at or before the time of Equity Funding's first public offering in 1964." [OJP][SECHist]
- **January 1965 – April 1973** — the conspiracy period charged in the federal indictment. [Wiki-EF][SEC-Digest]
- **January 1969** — co-founder Michael Riordan is killed when a mudslide smashes through his home in Mandeville Canyon, West LA, during a storm. Goldblum becomes sole leader. Riordan's brother Richard later becomes Mayor of Los Angeles. [Wiki-EF][Grok]
- **End of 1969** — EFCA is a full-blown financial conglomerate and one of Wall Street's favourite financial/insurance stocks. [OMF]
- **1969–1973** — EFCA mass-produces bogus policyholder records on its mainframe; roughly 64,000 fake policies are created (counts across sources: 56,000–64,000; face value $1.8–2.1 billion). Fake policies are coded "Department 99"/"Code 99" (no direct billing) and blocks of them are sold to reinsurers for cash. [Earmark][Decoded][Hancox][ACFE]
- **From 1970** — per Secrist's account in the court record, EFCA had been creating false insurance FILES (forged paper for auditors) since 1970, with the fake policies sold on to reinsurance companies. [DCCir]
- **"Cookie Jar Caper"** — EFCA executives reported deaths on some of the fake policies to collect death benefits from the reinsurers; at collapse it was rumoured a new program was being developed to "kill off" phantom policyholders on a regular schedule without arousing reinsurer suspicion. [Hancox][Decoded]
- **Early March 1973** — Ronald Secrist is fired/laid off from Bankers National, a New Jersey life insurer EFCA had acquired four years earlier. [DCCir]
- **March 6, 1973** — Secrist telephones Raymond Dirks, a New York insurance-stock analyst, saying he has information about fraud at Equity Funding. [DCCir]
- **March 7, 1973** — Secrist meets Dirks for several hours and lays out "detailed but nearly incredible" allegations. The same day/period he tells his whole story to the New York State Insurance Department. He deliberately avoids the SEC because he had heard the SEC had previously repeated employee tips back to Equity Funding's president. [DCCir][SCOTUS]
- **Mid-March 1973** — Dirks flies to Los Angeles, interviews EFCA officers and employees. Senior management denies everything; some employees corroborate the fraud. Throughout, Dirks urges William Blundell, the Wall Street Journal's LA bureau chief, to publish; Blundell declines — he "did not believe that such a massive fraud could go undetected" and feared libel. [SCOTUS]
- **March 12–27, 1973 (the two weeks)** — as Dirks investigates and word of Secrist's charges spreads among his institutional clients (who dump the stock), Equity Funding falls from $26 to under $15 per share. [SCOTUS]
- **March 27, 1973** — the New York Stock Exchange halts trading in Equity Funding. Shortly after, California insurance authorities impound EFCA records and uncover evidence of the fraud. Illinois insurance regulators are also involved in the investigation. [SCOTUS][Wiki-EF]
- **April 2, 1973** — only now does the SEC file its complaint against Equity Funding, and the Wall Street Journal runs a front-page story based largely on information assembled by Dirks. [SCOTUS]
- **April 5, 1973** — Equity Funding files for bankruptcy — at the time reported as the second-largest bankruptcy proceeding in US history; investor losses put at ~$300 million. [Liquidity][Fortune]
- **November 1, 1973** — a federal grand jury indicts 22 defendants on 105 counts: securities fraud, mail fraud, bank fraud, filing false documents with the SEC, interstate transportation of counterfeit securities — and electronic eavesdropping. The investigation was coordinated by the US Attorney in LA, SEC, US Postal Service, FBI, Federal Reserve Board, and the California and Illinois insurance departments. [OMF][SEC-Digest][Wiki-EF]
- **February 22 and October 31, 1974** — the bankruptcy Trustee files his reports — the basic factual record of the fraud. [SECHist]
- **October 9, 1974** — Goldblum pleads guilty to five felony counts. [search-result summary; verify — see uncertain_facts]
- **1975** — sentences: Goldblum 8 years + $20,000 fine; EVP Fred Levin 7 years; EVP-finance Samuel Lowell 5 years. In all, 22 people either plead guilty or are convicted. [MoneyWeek][OMF][Wiki-EF]
- **1976** — EFCA emerges from reorganization as Orion Capital Corporation (Orion later confirms in SEC filings that it is Equity Funding's successor). [EDGAR]
- **November 8, 1976** — the BBC broadcasts "The Billion Dollar Bubble" (made for the BBC, directed by Brian Gibson, starring a young James Woods as the actuary). [Wiki-BDB]
- **June 7, 1978** — Goldblum, still in prison for the fraud, files suit ~24 hours before NBC's scheduled US broadcast, trying to stop the film. He fails; NBC airs it in prime time on June 8, 1978. The Ninth Circuit rules against him (Goldblum v. NBC, 584 F.2d 904). [9thCir-NBC][Grok]
- **1981–82** — the SEC finds Dirks aided and abetted violations of Rule 10b-5 by telling clients, but — "because of his role in bringing the fraud to light" — imposes only a censure. The D.C. Circuit affirms (681 F.2d 824 (1982)). [SCOTUS][DCCir]
- **July 1, 1983** — the US Supreme Court reverses in Dirks v. SEC, No. 82-276: tippee liability requires that the insider breached a duty for personal benefit; Secrist and the other whistleblowing employees "were motivated by a desire to expose the fraud" and "received no monetary or personal benefit." Dirks is cleared after a decade. [SCOTUS][Oyez]
- **April 1984** — Fred Levin — who served 2 years and has changed his name to "Fred Evans" — is before a federal magistrate again, per Assistant US Attorney Leon Weidman. [UPI-84]
- **1996** — Stanley Goldblum, now controller of Primedex Corp (a California medical company), is charged along with the company's medical staff director with insurance fraud, illegal kickbacks to doctors, billing for services never performed, and ghostwriting medical reports. [Wiki-EF][FundingUniverse/Encyclopedia.com extracts]
- **August 31, 2011** — Stanley Goldblum dies in Beverly Hills, aged 84. [Legacy]
- **December 9, 2023** — Raymond Dirks dies in Manhattan, aged 89. NYT headline: "Raymond Dirks, Whose Tipster Case Redefined Insider Trading, Dies at 89." [NYT-obit][Legacy]

## 2. KEY PEOPLE

- **Stanley Goldblum** — chairman/CEO; ran the fraud from the top for nearly a decade; pleaded guilty, 8 years + $20,000 fine, served 4; sued NBC from prison to stop the TV movie; charged again in a medical-billing fraud at Primedex in the 1990s; died 2011, aged 84, in Beverly Hills. [MoneyWeek][9thCir-NBC][Legacy]
- **Michael Riordan** — co-founder and the company's charismatic other half; killed by a mudslide in his Mandeville Canyon home in January 1969; brother of future LA mayor Richard Riordan. [Wiki-EF][Grok]
- **Gordon C. McCormick** — devised the original (legal) mutual-fund-plus-life-insurance "funding program" concept and set up EFCA in 1960. [MoneyWeek][Decoded]
- **Fred Levin** — executive vice president over insurance operations, called by Fortune one of the "daring young con men"; 7-year sentence, served 2; resurfaced in 1984 under the new name "Fred Evans." [Fortune][OMF][UPI-84]
- **Samuel Lowell** — executive VP, finance; 5-year sentence. [OMF][SEC-Digest]
- **Art Lewis** — chief actuary, among the indicted; the character James Woods plays in "The Billion Dollar Bubble." [OMF][Wiki-BDB]
- **Alan Green** — the computer programmer among the 22 indicted — the man who made the mainframe give birth to fake people. [OMF]
- **Julian S. H. Weiner and Marvin Al Lichtig** — the audit-firm partner and manager among the indicted (Wolfson Weiner was EFCA's long-time audit firm). [SEC-Digest][OMF]
- **Ronald Secrist** — mid-level officer fired from EFCA subsidiary Bankers National in early March 1973; the whistleblower who deliberately bypassed the SEC (he'd heard it leaked employee tips back to EFCA's president) and instead told the New York State Insurance Department and analyst Ray Dirks. [DCCir]
- **Raymond Dirks** — 38-year-old (in 1973) New York broker-dealer officer and insurance-stock analyst; verified the fraud on the ground in LA; the SEC prosecuted him for it; vindicated by the Supreme Court in 1983; died 2023, aged 89. [SCOTUS][NYT-obit]
- **William Blundell** — WSJ Los Angeles bureau chief who sat on the story for three weeks fearing libel, then wrote the April 2, 1973 front-pager largely from Dirks's material. [SCOTUS]

## 3. THE NUMBERS

- **~64,000 fake policyholders** (sources range 56,000–64,000) with a **face value of ~$2 billion** — invented people carried on a real insurance company's books. [ACFE][Earmark][Wiki-EF]
- **~$2 billion in 1973 ≈ $14+ billion today** (inflation comparison for script — have the fact-checker run the exact CPI figure).
- **$25 million in counterfeit bonds** issued and **$100 million of company assets taken** by the time of the 1973 indictments. [ACFE]
- **~100 employees** engaged in the organised deception of investors, auditors, reinsurers and regulators from 1964 onward. [Wiki-EF]
- **22 defendants, 105 counts** in the November 1, 1973 federal indictment. [OMF][SEC-Digest]
- **$26 → under $15** — the stock's fall in the TWO WEEKS of Dirks's investigation, before trading was halted on March 27, 1973. [SCOTUS]
- **~$300 million** in investor losses; then the **second-largest bankruptcy proceeding in US history**. [Fortune]
- **9 years** — roughly how long the fraud ran (1964–1973) under the noses of auditors, reinsurers, two state insurance departments and the SEC. [OJP][Wiki-EF]
- **$20,000** — Goldblum's fine, against a ~$2 billion paper empire (a 0.001% ratio — jaw-drop contrast for the script). [MoneyWeek]
- **8 years sentenced, 4 served** (Goldblum); **7 sentenced, 2 served** (Levin). [MoneyWeek][UPI-84]
- **10 years** — how long it took the whistleblower's messenger, Dirks, to clear his own name (March 1973 → July 1, 1983). [SCOTUS]

## 4. DIRECT QUOTES (with attribution)

- "Dirks played an important role in bringing [Equity Funding's] massive fraud to light" — the SEC itself, in the very order censuring him (quoted in Dirks v. SEC, 463 U.S. 646 (1983)). [SCOTUS]
- "During the 2-week period in which Dirks pursued his investigation and spread word of Secrist's charges, the price of Equity Funding stock fell from $26 per share to less than $15 per share." — Dirks v. SEC, 463 U.S. 646 (1983). [SCOTUS]
- Blundell "did not believe that such a massive fraud could go undetected and declined to write the story," fearing that publishing "such damaging hearsay might be libelous." — the Supreme Court's account of the WSJ's refusal, Dirks v. SEC (1983). [SCOTUS]
- Secrist made "a series of detailed but nearly incredible allegations" — including that top officers "had Mafia connections which they used to threaten the lives of employees who objected," and that a major accounting firm (Haskins & Sells) had dropped the account over the company's practices. — Dirks v. SEC, 681 F.2d 824 (D.C. Cir. 1982). [DCCir]
- The whistleblowing employees "were motivated by a desire to expose the fraud" and "received no monetary or personal benefit for revealing Equity Funding's secrets." — Dirks v. SEC, 463 U.S. 646 (1983), on why there was no unlawful tip. [SCOTUS]
- "Those Daring Young Con Men of Equity Funding" — Fortune's 1973 headline. [Fortune]
- "The Equity Funding Papers: The Anatomy of a Fraud" — title of the 1974 book-length dissection catalogued in the DOJ's criminal-justice library. [OJP]

## 5. NARRATIVE ARC (three acts, 9–12 min)

**HOOK (cold open, 30–45s):** In 1973, an American insurance company was caught running the one scam a computer made possible and no human could: it *invented* tens of thousands of human beings — names, ages, medical histories, policies — sold them to other insurers for cash… and then started killing them off to collect the death benefits. Everyone in this story is real. Except 64,000 of them.

**ACT I — THE MACHINE (the rise, ~3 min):** 1960, Los Angeles. A genuinely clever legal product — buy mutual funds, borrow against them to pay life-insurance premiums — makes Equity Funding a Wall Street darling [MoneyWeek]. Charismatic co-founder Riordan dies in a freak 1969 mudslide, leaving Stanley Goldblum in sole command [Wiki-EF]. Growth targets can't be met honestly — so the mainframe starts printing people. Fake policies coded "Department 99," blocks of them sold to reinsurers for immediate cash [Earmark][Decoded]. But reinsurance is a treadmill: every fake policy sold means fake premiums owed next year — so the machine must invent MORE people every quarter. Eventually someone realises dead fake people are worth even more than living ones: the "Cookie Jar Caper" [Hancox]. When auditors sample files, staff forge them; roughly 100 employees are in on it [Wiki-EF]. The indictment will even include electronic eavesdropping [Wiki-EF].

**ACT II — THE TURN (the two weeks, ~4 min):** Early March 1973. Ronald Secrist, just fired from an EFCA subsidiary, decides to talk — but NOT to the SEC, because he'd heard the SEC leaked employee tips straight back to Equity Funding's president [DCCir]. He calls analyst Ray Dirks on March 6; next day, several hours of "detailed but nearly incredible" allegations — fake policies, Mafia threats, an audit firm that walked away [DCCir]. Dirks flies to LA. Management denies; employees confirm. The Wall Street Journal refuses to print it — too big to be true [SCOTUS]. But Dirks's fund-manager clients believe him and dump the stock: $26 → under $15 in two weeks [SCOTUS]. On March 27 the NYSE halts trading; California examiners impound the records and find the bodies — or rather, don't find them. On April 2 the SEC finally sues and the Journal finally runs its front page [SCOTUS]. Three days later: bankruptcy — then the second-largest in US history [Fortune][Liquidity].

**ACT III — THE RECKONING AND THE TWIST (~3–4 min):** 22 indicted on 105 counts [OMF]. Goldblum: 8 years and a $20,000 fine [MoneyWeek]. The twist that elevates this video: the SEC then went after… Ray Dirks — the man its own order admitted "played an important role in bringing the massive fraud to light" [SCOTUS]. Ten years of proceedings, up to the Supreme Court, which cleared him on July 1, 1983 and, in doing so, wrote the rule that still governs insider-trading tips today [SCOTUS][Oyez]. Ending beats: Goldblum sued NBC from his prison cell to stop the TV movie about him — and lost, the night before broadcast [9thCir-NBC]; out on parole, he surfaced decades later charged in ANOTHER fraud, at a medical company [Wiki-EF]; Levin changed his name [UPI-84]. Close on the AI-era resonance: in 1973 it took a mainframe, punch cards and a hundred accomplices to fake 64,000 people. Ask what it takes today.

## 6. SURPRISING DETAILS (primary-source gold)

- **The whistleblower didn't trust the SEC** — Secrist skipped the regulator because he'd heard it had repeated employee tips to Equity Funding's president; he went to a state insurance department and a stock analyst instead. [DCCir]
- **The Wall Street Journal turned down the scoop of the decade** for ~3 weeks — its LA bureau chief thought a fraud that big literally couldn't exist, and feared libel. [SCOTUS]
- **The indictment included electronic eavesdropping** — this wasn't just accounting fraud; among the 105 counts was bugging/wiretapping. [Wiki-EF][SEC-Digest]
- **$25 million in counterfeit bonds** — when fake people weren't enough, they printed fake securities. [ACFE]
- **They were building a system to 'kill' fake people on a schedule** — reported at collapse: a programme to kill off phantom policyholders regularly without arousing reinsurers' suspicion. [Hancox][Decoded]
- **Mafia death threats in a federal appellate opinion** — Secrist alleged top officers used claimed Mafia connections to threaten employees who objected. [DCCir]
- **The co-founder was killed by a mudslide** in his own Mandeville Canyon mansion in 1969 — and his brother later became Mayor of Los Angeles. [Wiki-EF][Grok]
- **The CEO sued a TV network from prison** to block "The Billion Dollar Bubble" 24 hours before airtime — creating a First Amendment case (Goldblum v. NBC) still cited on prior restraint. [9thCir-NBC]
- **A young James Woods** starred as the actuary in that 1976 BBC film — which is now free to watch on the Internet Archive. [Wiki-BDB]
- **The SEC's own censure order praised the man it was punishing** — "an important role in bringing the massive fraud to light." [SCOTUS]
- **The fraudsters' second acts**: Levin became "Fred Evans" and was back before a federal magistrate by 1984 [UPI-84]; Goldblum resurfaced as controller of Primedex, charged in a 1990s medical-billing fraud [Wiki-EF].
- **The fraud outlived its own detection risk for 9 years** because the auditors' samples were forged to order — and the audit firm's own partner and manager ended up among the indicted. [SEC-Digest][OMF]

## 7. WHAT HAPPENED AFTER

- **Equity Funding** → reorganized (bankruptcy filed April 5, 1973; Trustee reports 1974) and emerged in 1976 as **Orion Capital Corporation**, which lived on as a real insurer into the late 1990s. [Liquidity][SECHist][EDGAR]
- **Stanley Goldblum** → 8 years, served 4; sued NBC (lost); later controller of Primedex Corp, charged in 1996 medical-insurance-fraud case; died August 31, 2011, Beverly Hills, aged 84. [MoneyWeek][9thCir-NBC][Wiki-EF][Legacy]
- **Fred Levin** → 7 years, served 2; changed his name to Fred Evans; before a federal magistrate again in April 1984. [UPI-84]
- **Raymond Dirks** → censured 1981, affirmed by D.C. Circuit 1982, **vindicated 6–3 by the Supreme Court July 1, 1983** (opinion by Justice Powell; Blackmun dissent — see uncertain_facts for vote detail). Kept working on Wall Street for decades; died December 9, 2023, aged 89. The "personal benefit" test from his case still defines insider-trading law. [SCOTUS][Oyez][NYT-obit]
- **Ronald Secrist** → largely vanished from the public record after testifying — an honest afternote for the script: the man who triggered everything is the one history forgot.
- **The profession** → the scandal became a set-text in auditing ("one of auditing's darkest hours" — Gale/Internal Auditor article title), a GAO lessons-learned report, an ACFE case study, a Fortune classic, a book ("The Equity Funding Papers: The Anatomy of a Fraud", 1974) and a BBC film. [GAO][OJP][ACFE][Fortune][Wiki-BDB]
